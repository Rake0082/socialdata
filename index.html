Did New York City Keep Its Promise?

In 2014, New York City launched Vision Zero — a pledge to eliminate all traffic deaths. Over a decade later, the data tells a complicated story.

Data source: NYC Open Data — Motor Vehicle Collisions  |  02806 Social Data Analysis  |  2026

Introduction

On January 1st, 2014, New York City Mayor Bill de Blasio launched Vision Zero — an ambitious initiative borrowed from Sweden, with a simple but radical premise: no traffic death is acceptable. The city committed to redesigning streets, lowering speed limits, and intensifying enforcement, all in pursuit of a single goal: zero fatalities.

This story draws on over 2.2 million crash records from NYC Open Data, spanning July 2012 to early 2026. Every crash reported to the NYPD is in this dataset — from minor fender-benders to fatal collisions. We focus on what matters most: did people die, and has that number gone down?

14 years of traffic deaths in New York City

Between 2012 and 2025, 3,523 people were killed in traffic crashes across New York City's five boroughs. The trend is not a straight line — it bends, dips, and rises again in ways that reflect policy, pandemic, and human behaviour.

The baseline: before Vision Zero (2012–2013)

In the two years before Vision Zero, New York City recorded between 270 and 300 traffic deaths per year. Pedestrians accounted for the majority — over half — of all fatalities. These numbers set the bar that Vision Zero promised to drive to zero.

Vision Zero launches — and it works, at first

From 2014 onward, the death toll began falling steadily. The city lowered the default speed limit from 30 to 25 mph, redesigned dozens of dangerous intersections, and ramped up speeding enforcement. By 2019, NYC recorded its lowest traffic death count since records began — a genuine, measurable success.

COVID-19 changes everything (2020)

The pandemic emptied New York's streets almost overnight. Total crashes dropped sharply — but fatalities did not fall proportionally. With fewer cars and no congestion, speeds rose dramatically, and the crashes that did happen were more deadly. 2020 saw a sharp reversal in the decade's downward trend.

The promise stalls — still far from zero

Since 2021, traffic deaths have stabilised — but at a level far above zero. The progress of the 2010s has not been recovered. Over a decade after the Vision Zero pledge, New York City still loses more than 200 people a year to traffic violence. The promise remains unfinished.

The decline from 2014 to 2019 is real and significant — Vision Zero's infrastructure and enforcement changes saved lives. But the gains have since stalled, and the city remains far from its founding promise.

Not all boroughs are equal

Traffic fatalities are not evenly distributed across New York City's five boroughs. Brooklyn and Queens account for the largest absolute numbers — but when adjusted for population and traffic volume, the picture shifts. The map shows total fatalities per borough over the full period.

The deadliest streets

Zoom into the crash-level data and patterns emerge around specific corridors — major arterials like Atlantic Avenue, Queens Boulevard (long nicknamed the "Boulevard of Death"), and stretches of the Bronx. These are the streets where Vision Zero's redesign investments have been concentrated — and where the most lives are still being lost.

A city of unequal risk

The geographic concentration of traffic deaths follows familiar patterns. Lower-income neighbourhoods bear a disproportionate share of traffic fatalities — a pattern documented in Vision Zero's own equity reports, and one the initiative has struggled to fully address.

Figure 3: Who Bears the Risk?

Not everyone faces the same danger on New York City's streets. Pedestrians account for the largest share of traffic fatalities — consistently over half of all deaths in most years. Cyclists, though fewer in number, face a disproportionate risk relative to their share of road users. Motorists, despite being the most numerous, die at a proportionally lower rate.

Conclusion

The data tells a complicated story about Vision Zero's first decade in New York City. The years from 2014 to 2019 represent genuine progress: infrastructure investments, lower speed limits, and targeted enforcement produced a measurable decline in traffic deaths, reaching a historic low in 2019. These were real lives saved.

But the COVID-19 pandemic disrupted the trajectory in ways the city has not fully recovered from. With emptier streets came higher speeds and more deadly crashes — and since 2021, the death toll has stabilised at levels far above the 2019 low. Over a decade after the pledge, New York City still records over 200 traffic deaths per year. Zero remains a distant aspiration.

The geographic patterns are equally sobering. Traffic deaths cluster in specific corridors and neighbourhoods — many of them lower-income — suggesting that the benefits of Vision Zero's street redesigns have not been equally distributed. Pedestrians remain the most vulnerable group, accounting for the majority of fatalities year after year.

Vision Zero was always a long-term project. The question is whether the political will and sustained investment exist to finish it.

San Francisco Crime Data · 2003–2024

Drug & Narcotic Crime in San Francisco

A data story about how drug-related crime has evolved across the city — where it happens, when it peaks, and what the numbers hide.

Data source: SF Police Department  |  02806 Social Data Analysis  |  2026

Introduction

San Francisco has long been at the center of debates around drug policy, homelessness, and public safety. This story explores drug and narcotic crime as recorded by the San Francisco Police Department between 2003 and 2024. The data comes from SFPD incident reports — official records filed each time an officer records a drug-related event — and covers over two decades of policing across the city's 10 districts.

It is important to note that these numbers reflect reported and recorded incidents — not drug use itself. Changes in the data may reflect shifts in police enforcement priorities just as much as actual changes in drug activity. A drop in incidents does not mean drugs disappeared; it may simply mean fewer officers were looking.

The full picture: 2003–2024

Drug and narcotic incidents in San Francisco tell a complex story. Over two decades, the number of reported incidents has fluctuated dramatically — shaped by policy, policing, and public health crises.

A peak in the mid-2000s

Reported incidents reached their highest levels around 2008–2009, with over 6,000 cases per year. This period coincided with intensive street-level drug enforcement in districts like the Tenderloin and Mission.

The sharp decline after 2009

After 2009, numbers dropped steeply. This reflects both a shift in SFPD enforcement priorities and broader policy changes around drug possession. Fewer arrests does not necessarily mean less drug activity.

COVID-19 and the 2020 drop

The pandemic year saw a dramatic dip in reported incidents — a pattern seen across almost all crime categories. Reduced police patrols and lockdowns meant fewer street-level drug encounters were recorded.

A recent uptick

From 2021 onward, drug-related incidents began rising again — partly reflecting the fentanyl crisis that has hit San Francisco particularly hard, with the Tenderloin and SoMa districts seeing significant increases.

The trend above shows reported incidents, not drug use. The data is a lens into how the city has chosen — or been forced — to respond to drug activity over time.

The city as a whole

Across all 10 police districts, drug and narcotic crime is not evenly distributed. The map shows each district's ratio compared to the city average — values above 1 mean a disproportionately high share of drug incidents.

The Tenderloin stands out

With a ratio of 4.3, the Tenderloin has more than four times the citywide average share of drug incidents. No other district comes close. The Mission (1.4) and Southern (1.1) districts also exceed average, but the gap with the Tenderloin is stark.

A small neighborhood, an outsized presence

The Tenderloin is one of San Francisco's smallest districts by area — yet it accounts for a disproportionate share of all drug-related policing. Whether this reflects genuine concentration of drug activity or concentrated enforcement is a question the data alone cannot answer.

Conclusion

Three patterns emerge clearly from two decades of SFPD drug incident data. First, reported drug crime peaked around 2008–2009 and has fallen sharply since — a decline that coincides with policy shifts, not necessarily with reduced drug use. Second, the Tenderloin has been the dominant site of drug enforcement throughout the entire period, with a ratio more than four times the city average. Third, and perhaps most striking: even as the city's total numbers fell, the Tenderloin's share of drug incidents grew — suggesting that enforcement became increasingly concentrated in one small neighborhood while easing elsewhere.

What does this mean? The data tells us where and when police recorded drug activity — it does not tell us where drug use actually occurs. As Richardson et al. (2019) note, crime data reflects enforcement patterns as much as criminal behavior. A neighborhood that is heavily policed will produce more incident reports, regardless of whether drug activity there is genuinely higher than in less-policed areas. The Tenderloin's outsized numbers may reflect a real concentration of open drug markets — but they also reflect decades of concentrated policing in one of the city's most vulnerable neighborhoods.

The recent uptick from 2021 is consistent with the fentanyl crisis that has hit San Francisco particularly hard, with overdose deaths surpassing COVID-19 deaths in the city in 2021 (SFDPH, 2022). Whether the data captures the true scale of this crisis — or only the portion that intersects with law enforcement — remains an open question.
